The KV Cache

The trick that makes a chatbot answer at a readable speed is also the reason it runs out of memory.

Published

13 August 2026

Read time

34 min read

Tags

Foundations for LLM

I want to explain one of the most consequential ideas in how large language models are actually run. There is no famous paper behind it. Nobody won a prize for it, and as far as I can tell nobody ever claimed it as a contribution. It is in every serious implementation of a language model, it is the reason a chatbot can answer you at a speed you are willing to sit through, and it is also the single largest reason that running these models is expensive and awkward.

It is called the KV cache. By the end of this post I would like you to be able to say exactly what is stored in it, why storing it is allowed at all, how many bytes it takes, and why the people who run language models for a living spend so much of their time worrying about it. I am going to assume you know nothing about how a language model works on the inside, so that is where we will start.

The Machine That Starts Over at Every Word

Let me begin with something that surprised me when I first understood it properly: a language model has no memory.

When you type a question into a chatbot and it writes you an answer, it feels as though you are talking to something that is following the conversation. I am afraid you are not, and I still find this a little counterintuitive even though I know it perfectly well. The thing underneath is a mathematical function in the strict sense. You hand it a sequence of text, and it hands you back a probability for every possible word that might come next. That is the whole of what it does. It keeps no notebook between calls. Everything it appears to remember about your conversation is there because the whole conversation was handed to it again, from the beginning, as part of the input.

I want to flag that sentence, because we are going to contradict it later and I would rather you saw it coming. This post is about a buffer that the model does keep between calls. The reason that is not a contradiction is that nothing in the buffer is new information: every byte of it could be recomputed from the text alone. It is a cache, in the ordinary sense of something kept only to avoid doing work twice, and not a memory.

I should be more careful about the word "word". Language models do not work in words but in tokens, which are chunks of text somewhere between a character and a word. The word "cache" might be one token; an unusual word like "Kaiqian" might be broken into three or four. Nothing in this post depends on the details of how that chunking is done, so if you prefer to imagine words wherever I write "tokens", you will not go wrong.

So if the model only ever predicts one token, how does it produce a whole paragraph?

It calls the function again. Let me be concrete about what that means, since this is the mechanism everything else rests on. The model takes the token it just produced, glues it onto the end of the text it was given, and feeds the whole thing back in as if it were a fresh question. Then it does it again. And again. A model writing a hundred tokens of prose is a function that has been called a hundred times, each time on a sequence one token longer than the last.

Figure 1. Writing four tokens means calling the model four times. The new token at the end of each line is the only thing that call produces, and everything to its left is text the model has already been shown. I would ask you to notice how much of each line is simply a repeat of the line above it, because that repetition is what this whole post is about.

I would like you to sit with that picture for a moment, because the whole of the rest of this post falls out of it. Every call receives everything the previous call received, plus one token. By the time the model is writing the thousandth token of an answer, it is being handed a sequence of a thousand tokens and asked to think about all of them, when nine hundred and ninety-nine of them are exactly the same as they were a moment ago.

The obvious question is whether the model really has to think about all of them again from scratch. The answer is no, and the KV cache is how it avoids doing so. But to see what can be saved and what cannot, we have to look inside one of those calls.

1. A Trick Nobody Published, and the Bill That Came Later

Before we go inside, here is the shape of the story, because it is a slightly unusual one for a technical idea.

The first box is the odd one. There is no citation for it because nobody ever claimed it as a contribution. The technique is written down in plenty of places — Shazeer's 2019 paper, which we will come to, prints the algorithm as pseudocode — but always as setup for something else, the way you might mention that you compiled your code before running it. It was too obvious to publish and too useful to skip.

What changes in 2019 is not the trick but the diagnosis. Shazeer looked at why generating text was slow and concluded that the problem was not the amount of arithmetic being done. It was, in his words, "the memory-bandwidth cost of repeatedly loading the large “keys” and “values” tensors". I will unpack that sentence properly later, because it turns out to be the whole story of section 4. For now it is enough to notice the shape of it: the cache started life as a saving and became a cost.

2. What Each Word Asks, and What It Offers

To see what is worth keeping, we need to know what the model computes when it reads a sequence. The mechanism is called attention. I have written a whole post about it — Attention — and if you want the derivations and the history, that is where they are. But I am not going to send you away to understand this one, so here is everything you actually need, built up from the beginning.

Start with the problem attention solves, because the mechanism makes far more sense once you know what it is for. The meaning of a word depends on the words around it. In "the cat sat on the mat", the word "sat" means what it does because of "cat". So each token needs a way to look at the other tokens and draw in whatever it needs from them.

Every token is first turned into a vector, which is simply an ordered list of numbers — a few thousand of them — that stands for what the model understands about that token. Attention then gives each token three such vectors rather than one. The first says what that token is looking for. The second advertises what it has to offer, so other tokens can judge whether they want it. The third is the content that gets handed over if they do. These are called the query, the key, and the value, and the names come from databases, where you match a query against a key to retrieve a value.

I find an analogy helps here, as long as you do not lean on it too hard. Imagine every token pins a small notice to a board saying what it is about; that notice is its key. Every token also walks along the board holding a description of what it wants, which is its query. Where a query matches a notice, that token takes a copy of whatever is behind it — the value — and a token that matches several notices takes some of each, in proportion.

Attention is then a weighted average, computed in two steps. First the current token's query is compared against every key by taking a dot product: multiply the two lists together entry by entry and add up the results. That gives one score per token so far, including the current one. A dot product is large when the two vectors point in similar directions, and larger still when they are long, so the score says roughly how well that token matches what the current one is looking for.

Those scores can be any size, positive or negative, and what we need is proportions. So they are pushed through a function called the softmax, which raises $e$ to the power of each score and then divides by the total. Everything that comes out is positive, and the results add up to one, which is exactly what lets us treat them as the proportions of a blend. Those scores are turned into weights that add up to one, and the weights are used to average the value vectors together:

$$ \text{Attention}(q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) V . $$

The division by $\sqrt{d_h}$ is there to stop the scores growing as the vectors get wider. Without it the softmax collapses onto a single token and the model becomes very hard to train, which I take apart properly in the other post.

One note on symbols, because I am about to use two that look alike. I will write $d_h$ for the width of a single head's vectors — how many numbers are in one key — and plain $d$ for the full width of the model, which is all the heads laid end to end. In Llama 3 8B, $d_h$ is 128 and $d$ is 4,096. Section 4 uses $d$; this section used $d_h$.

Two structural facts and we can move on. The first is that the model does not do this once across the full width of its vectors. It splits them into several smaller pieces and runs the whole procedure on each piece independently, with its own learned projections. These pieces are called heads, and they let the model attend to several different things at once. Because the width is split rather than duplicated, thirty-two heads cost about the same as one full-width head, which is worth knowing if you were about to assume otherwise. The second fact is that the model repeats the entire arrangement at every layer, stacked dozens deep — Llama 3 70B has 80 of them. So whenever I say "the cache" below, I mean a separate one of these in every layer.

The one thing to carry forward is this. Of the three vectors, the query belongs to the moment: it is the question this token is asking right now. The key and the value belong to the token itself, and everything that comes later is going to want them.

Figure 2. This is where the cache actually lives. I have drawn one attention layer at the moment a new token arrives. The token is projected three ways, but only the key and value get appended to the two stores. The query is used once, right here, to look over every row that has ever been written, and is then discarded. Remember that there is one of these pictures in every layer of the model.

3. The Rows That Never Change Again

Now we can see the waste clearly, and it is embarrassing.

Let me take you back to Figure 1, to the call that produces "mat". That call is handed "the cat sat on the", and it computes a query, a key and a value for every one of those five tokens. But the call before it was handed "the cat sat on" and computed a query, a key and a value for each of those four. The key for "the", the key for "cat", the key for "sat" — the model computed all of them a moment ago, threw them away, and has just computed them again. The same numbers.

So keep them. That really is the entire idea, and I remember being faintly disappointed when I first understood it, because something with such large consequences ought to be more complicated than this. After computing the key and value for a token, we put them in a buffer and hold on to them, so that the next call can use them instead of computing them a second time. That buffer is the KV cache.

Concretely, for a standard attention layer, the cache is two tensors:

$$ K_{\text{cache}},\; V_{\text{cache}} \in \mathbb{R}^{\,n_{kv} \times t \times d_h}, $$

where $n_{kv}$ is the number of heads that keep keys and values, $t$ is the number of tokens seen so far, and $d_h$ is the length of each vector. I find it easiest to read that as a stack of rows: one row per token, for each head, in each of the two tensors. Every new token adds exactly one row to each. There is one of these pairs in every layer, and one for every conversation being served.

Let me show you it filling up. Below is a walkthrough of four tokens, with two heads and vectors of length four — absurdly small compared to a real model, which is the point, because you can see every number. The first two tokens are the prompt and the last two are generated. Step through it and watch the rows accumulate.

Figure 3. Four tokens, two heads, and vectors of length four. I have drawn this at an absurdly small scale on purpose, so that every number is on the page. Each step adds one new row to each cache, and every earlier row is reused untouched. The bars are real softmax weights, computed in your browser from the vectors beside them.

The counter underneath is the part I would ask you to watch. With a cache, reaching the fourth token costs four rows of work. Without one it costs nine, because the model would have redone the whole prefix at every step. Four against nine is not very impressive on its own, but that gap grows with the square of the length of the answer, and answers get long.

Why the Queries Are Not Kept

You may have noticed that I said the model computes three vectors per token but the cache stores two. The queries are thrown away, and this is my favourite thing in the whole topic, because the reason is so clean.

Think of attention as a question that the present asks of the past. When the model is working on token $t$, it takes that token's query and compares it against the keys of every token up to and including $t$. It never uses the query belonging to token 3 again after step 3 has finished. Token 3's key and value, by contrast, will be consulted at step 4, and step 5, and every step after that until the conversation ends.

So the asymmetry in the cache is the asymmetry in the question. A token's question is asked once and is never asked again. A token's answer has to stay available forever, because everything that comes later is going to ask it something.

Why We Are Allowed to Do This at All

I have been assuming something without saying so, and I want to make it explicit, because it is the assumption the whole technique stands on. Keeping old values only works if they are still correct. It would be no use caching the key for "cat" if appending the word "mat" quietly changed what that key should have been. So why doesn't it?

In a model of this kind, the key and value for token $i$ are computed from token $i$ and the tokens before it, and from nothing else. This is a design choice called causal masking: when the model processes a position, it is forbidden from looking at anything to the right of it. The reason for that choice has nothing to do with caching — it is there so the model cannot cheat during training by peeking at the answer — but it has the happy consequence that appending a token can never reach backwards and change a row that has already been written.

I want to use this to test a rule you will hear repeated often, which is that the KV cache only works in decoders. I think that is nearly right and the reason usually given for it is wrong, which makes it worth taking apart carefully.

A bidirectional encoder, of the kind used in models like BERT, lets every token look at every other token in both directions. Add a word to the end and every representation in the sequence changes, because every one of them was allowed to depend on the whole sequence. There is nothing stable to keep. That much supports the rule.

But now I want you to consider a translation model, which has an encoder for the input sentence and a decoder that writes the output. When the decoder attends to the encoder's output — this is called cross-attention — those keys and values are cached, in every real implementation. And cross-attention is bidirectional. So the rule cannot really be about masking.

What I think the rule is actually about is finality. A row is worth caching when nothing that arrives later can change it. Causal masking is the usual way to guarantee that, and it is not the only one: the encoder's output is final too, not because it is masked, but because the encoder has already finished running.

Figure 4. I would move the toggle before reading on. With the mask on, every row already in the cache stays valid when a new token arrives, because none of those rows was ever allowed to see it. Turn the mask off and all of them go stale at once, because each was computed from a sequence that has just changed.

Two Phases That Behave Nothing Alike

There is one more distinction I need to give you before we can talk about cost, and I think it explains most of what is strange about running these models.

Answering a question happens in two phases that behave nothing alike. The first is prefill: the model reads your entire prompt. It can do this in one go, because all of the prompt is available at once, and computers are very good at doing the same operation to a thousand things simultaneously. Prefill fills the cache and it keeps the hardware busy.

The second is decode: the model writes the answer, one token at a time, because it cannot start the second token until it has seen the first. Each step does a small amount of arithmetic, and reads the entire cache in order to attend over it. Prefill is the model reading; decode is the model writing, and writing is much harder to make fast.

How Big This Gets

We can now compute the size of the thing. Multiply out the shapes: for $L$ layers, at $p$ bytes for each number,

$$ \text{bytes} \;=\; 2 \, L \, n_{kv} \, d_h \, t \, p . $$

The 2 is because we keep both a key and a value. Everything else is a count of how many numbers there are.

Let us put real figures in it. Llama 3 8B, one of the smaller open models, has $L = 32$ layers, $n_{kv} = 8$ key-value heads, and $d_h = 128$. Models generally store each number in 16 bits rather than the 32 that used to be standard, because half the precision turns out to be plenty and it halves the memory. Sixteen bits is two bytes, so $p = 2$. Multiplying it out: $2 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 131{,}072$ bytes.

That is 128 KiB. I should explain the unit, since it trips people up: memory is counted in powers of two, so a kibibyte (KiB) is 1,024 bytes rather than 1,000, and 131,072 is exactly $128 \times 1{,}024$. Cache sizes from here on are in those units. Confusingly, GPUs are sold in decimal gigabytes — an "80 GB" H100 holds 80 billion bytes, which is about 74.5 GiB — so the calculator in the next section reports GB to match the hardware.

I want to let that number sit for a second, because it is per token. Every token in the conversation, whether you typed it or the model wrote it, costs 128 KiB of memory that somebody has to hold onto for as long as the conversation lasts. Llama 3 70B, with its 80 layers, costs 320 KiB per token. Models have a limit on how much text they can consider at once, called the context window, and for Llama 3.1 that limit is 128,000 tokens. Fill it and you are holding 40 GiB of cache. That is for one conversation, with one user.

That number is why the rest of this post exists.

4. One Room per Guest, and the Weights Everyone Shares

I have been describing the cache as a saving, which it is. Now I want to describe what it costs, because the cost turns out to be more interesting.

Let me start with the thing that makes this cost an unusual one. The weights of a model — the numbers learned during training — are shared. Every conversation being served uses the same weights, so if a server is handling fifty conversations at once, it reads those weights once and lets all fifty benefit. The cache is the opposite. Each conversation has its own, and it cannot be shared with anyone else unless two conversations happen to begin with exactly the same text.

To see why that matters, we need one fact about the hardware. Models are run on GPUs — graphics processors, originally built for rendering, which happen to be very good at doing the same arithmetic to thousands of numbers at once. On a GPU, moving a number out of memory and into the part of the chip that does the arithmetic takes time, and it takes far more time than the arithmetic itself does. An NVIDIA H100 can perform roughly three hundred arithmetic operations in the time it takes to fetch a single byte from its memory. The rate at which it can move bytes is called its memory bandwidth, and it is exactly what Shazeer was pointing at in the sentence I quoted in section 1. So a computation that does very little arithmetic per byte fetched leaves the chip idle, waiting for memory to arrive.

This is exactly what Shazeer worked out in 2019. He computed the ratio of memory access to arithmetic for one decoding step and got

$$ \Theta\!\left(\frac{n}{d} + \frac{1}{b}\right), $$

where $n$ is the length of the sequence, $d$ is the width of the model, and $b$ is the number of conversations being served together. (The estimate assumes $n \le d$; longer sequences only make the first term worse.) Now think about what a ratio close to one would mean. The chip would be moving one byte out of memory for every single arithmetic operation it performs. That is a terrible trade on this hardware. As I said a moment ago, an H100 can do about three hundred operations in the time it takes to fetch one byte, so at a ratio of one it is spending almost all of its time waiting for memory to arrive and almost none of it computing. The machine is idling, and you are still paying for it by the hour.

Now I would like you to look at the two terms separately, because they behave completely differently and the difference is the entire problem. The $1/b$ term is the friendly one: serve more conversations at once and it shrinks, since the weights get read once and shared. This is why servers batch requests together. The $n/d$ term is the unfriendly one. It does not shrink with batching at all, because every conversation drags its own cache along. Adding a fifty-first conversation adds a fifty-first cache to be read.

You can feel the trade in the figure below. Move the batch size and watch the throughput climb as the weights get amortised. Then push the context length out and watch the cache take over the bar underneath, until the curve stops rising and the machine simply runs out of memory.

Figure 5. A Llama 3 8B-shaped model on one H100. I should say plainly that this assumes peak bandwidth and peak arithmetic throughput, so it is an upper bound on a machine nobody actually has; a real server lands well below it. The bar splits the bytes each step has to move into weights, read once for the whole batch, and cache, read once per conversation. Nothing anywhere in this range is limited by arithmetic.

The field's first answer was to make the cache smaller, by having several query heads share a single key-value head between them. That is the story of multi-query and grouped-query attention, and it deserves its own post rather than a paragraph in this one.

But there is a second failure that no amount of shrinking would have fixed, and I think it is the more interesting one, because nothing about it is a property of the model at all. The attention kernels of the day — a kernel being the small, heavily optimised program that actually runs one piece of the model on the GPU — required the cache for a single conversation to sit in one unbroken block of memory. Nobody knows in advance how long a conversation will run. So systems did the only safe thing, and reserved each conversation enough room for the longest answer it might ever produce.

Figure 6. Four conversations growing in one strip of memory. The solid cells are holding real keys and values. The pale ones have been reserved for text that has not arrived yet, and I would watch how many of them there are, because every one is memory that nobody else is allowed to use.

The consequence is that most of the memory set aside for caches was holding nothing at all. The waste has a name, or rather two: internal fragmentation is the room reserved inside a conversation's block that it never grows into, and external fragmentation is the gaps left between blocks that are too small for anyone else to use. In 2023 Kwon and colleagues measured this on real traffic, and the result is the reason vLLM exists.

Figure 7. Reproduced from Kwon et al. (2023), Figure 2, and recoloured into this site's palette. Each bar is the whole of one system's KV cache memory, and only the bottom band is holding real tokens: between 20.4% and 38.2% of it in the systems of the day, against 96.3% once the memory is handed out in blocks. I would stress that no tensor got any smaller here.

I find this figure quietly remarkable. The fix was not a better model, a smaller cache, or a cleverer attention formula. It was to stop insisting that the cache be contiguous, and instead hand it out in small blocks as it is needed, which is exactly what operating systems have done with memory for fifty years. Nothing got smaller. It was simply put away properly.

There is one last cost, and it is different in kind from the others, so I want to be careful about how I say it. Paging the cache, batching conversations together, reading the rows in a different order — none of these changes a single output. The model answers exactly as it would have done otherwise, and that is what has made every trick in this section free.

The techniques that come next are not like that, and it is worth knowing where the line is. Throwing away old tokens to save room, or storing the cache in four bits instead of sixteen, changes what the model computes. Sharing key-value heads, which I mentioned a few paragraphs ago, is a third case again: it does not approximate an existing model, it produces a different model that has to be trained or retrained to work that way. All three may be worth doing. None of them is free in the way that paging was.

5. Fifteen Decimal Places, and a Floor I Did Not Expect

I did not want to take either of the two central claims on trust, so I wrote the experiment. It decodes 320 tokens through one real attention layer, twice: once keeping a cache, and once recomputing the whole prefix at every step, the way a model with no cache would have to. Both paths do genuine arithmetic on genuine vectors, and I timed each step of both.

The first claim to check is that caching does not change the answer. It should not — we argued earlier that the rows are final — but arguing and checking are different activities. Over all 320 steps, the largest disagreement between the cached output and the recomputed one was $2.2 \times 10^{-15}$, which is the noise floor of the arithmetic itself. The cache is algebraically exact, which is the sense that matters: it changes no term in the definition of attention. (Two different implementations on a real GPU, working in 16-bit arithmetic, will still disagree in the last few bits. That is because floating-point addition is not associative, so adding the same numbers in a different order gives a very slightly different answer. It is a property of the arithmetic rather than of the cache.)

The second claim is about how much work is saved, and here we can be exact, so let me count it out rather than assert it. I will count multiply-and-add operations, and I will use $d$ for the full width of the model, not the per-head $d_h$ from section 2.

A cached step does two things. First it projects the one new token, which means multiplying its vector of length $d$ by each of four matrices — the query, key and value projections, and the output projection afterwards. Each matrix has $d^2$ entries and each entry costs a multiply and an add, so those four come to $8d^2$. Then it attends over the $t$ rows already sitting in the cache: one dot product of length $d$ against each of $t$ keys, and then a weighted sum of $t$ value vectors of length $d$. That is $4td$. So a cached step costs $8d^2 + 4td$.

A step without a cache does the very same work, but for all $t$ tokens instead of one. Projecting them all costs $8td^2$, and attending over the full $t \times t$ grid of scores costs $4t^2d$. Now put the two next to each other. Every term in the second carries exactly one factor of $t$ that its counterpart in the first does not, so the second is $t$ times the first — exactly, not approximately. At the 320th token, a model without a cache is doing three hundred and twenty times the arithmetic to produce the very same row of numbers. (That is against this particular baseline, which recomputes the full square of scores; an implementation that exploited the mask would do about half as much, so call it between $t/2$ and $t$.)

Figure 8. My own measurements, from figures/kvcache.py. In (a) the no-cache points land on the curve their arithmetic predicts, while the with-cache points sit flat and far above theirs — that gap is the surprise I describe below. (b) is the bill for the whole sequence. Do note that these are wall-clock times on my laptop, so the 11$\times$ will move on your machine.

Panel (a) is the one I did not expect, and it turned out to be the most instructive thing in the experiment. The blue points, the ones without a cache, land neatly on the curve their arithmetic predicts. The lavender points, with the cache, refuse to. They sit flat, and far above their own prediction — roughly ten times higher than the FLOP count says they should be.

The reason is section 4 arriving early, on a laptop, without my arranging it. A cached step has so little arithmetic to do that the arithmetic is no longer what is being paid for. What is being paid for is the fixed cost of setting the work up at all, and one token is nowhere near enough to keep the machine busy. The cache removes the computation problem so thoroughly that a memory and overhead problem is all that is left standing. That is the whole of modern inference engineering in one accidental measurement.

6. Chat This Over With Friends

A language model remembers nothing between words. Every token it writes is a fresh call on the entire conversation so far, and if it really started from scratch each time, the cost of an answer would grow with the square of its length. The KV cache is the fix, and it is almost embarrassingly simple: for each token, the model computes a key and a value once and then keeps them, because in a causal model nothing that arrives later can change them. The part I would lead with at a table is what is not kept. Each token also produces a query, and the queries are thrown away — a token's question is asked once and never again, while its answer has to stay available forever. In a 70B model each token costs about 320 kilobytes of this, so a single long conversation quietly accumulates 40 gigabytes of cache, for one person.

The version people repeat is that the KV cache is a decoder-only trick. That is close, but the reason is not the name of the block: translation models cache their cross-attention keys quite happily even though cross-attention looks in both directions, while a bidirectional encoder can cache nothing at all, because appending a word rewrites every representation it had. What makes a row keepable is simply that nothing arriving later can change it. The fair objection to my account is that calling the cache the bottleneck overstates it — at a large batch with a short context, the weights still dominate the traffic and the cache is not what binds — so the honest claim is narrower: it binds at long context, which happens to be where everyone now operates. What is genuinely unsettled is the next move. Evicting old tokens and storing the cache in four bits both change what the model computes, and so they give up the one property that made every earlier trick free.
