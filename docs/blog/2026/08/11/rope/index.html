RoPE

Position becomes an angle, the angle cancels, and what is left is a bank of clocks that the whole field is now busy retuning.

Published

11 August 2026

Read time

10 min read

Tags

Foundations for LLM

Attention has no built-in sense of order: shuffle the tokens and the outputs shuffle with them. If a model is to know that "dog bites man" differs from "man bites dog", you have to arrange it. Almost every open model now arranges it with rotary position embedding, which adds nothing to the token vector — it rotates it, by an angle proportional to position, chosen so the absolute angles cancel when two tokens are compared.

A Rotation That Is Thrown Away

In its original formulation RoPE is applied inside each attention head, to queries and keys only, just before the dot product. It never touches the values or the residual stream: the rotation happens to a copy, is used once, and is gone — unlike an additive tag, which travels through the whole network. "Original formulation" does real work there: GPT-J rotates a quarter of each head's dimensions, DeepSeek's latent attention a dedicated subset, Llama 4 three layers in four. RoPE also has no learned parameters, and section 3 is how that eroded.

1. Four Answers, and One of Them Is Silence

Four ways to tell a model where a token sits, in the order tried. The arrow labels are what each was still paying.

The 2021 question is the sharp one: is there an $f$ writing absolute position into a vector such that the dot product depends only on the relative one?

Adoption, stated carefully. Among dense open-weight decoder-only models since 2023 RoPE is close to universal — but not universal: BLOOM and MPT use ALiBi, Jamba nothing, and Mamba and RWKV are not attention models.

2. The Angle That Cancels

Start with two dimensions. With $R(\alpha)$ a rotation and $q = W_q x_m$, $k = W_k x_n$ the projected query and key, RoPE sets $q_m = R(m\theta)q$ and $k_n = R(n\theta)k$. Take the dot product, using the two facts that make rotations special — $R(\alpha)^\top = R(-\alpha)$, and that they compose by adding angles:

$$ q_m^\top k_n = q^\top R(-m\theta)R(n\theta)\,k = q^\top R\big((n-m)\theta\big)\,k . $$

The absolute positions have cancelled, exactly, by the group structure of rotation.

Where does the rotation come from? Read the pair as a complex number with a magnitude and a phase: for the inner product to depend only on $n-m$, the phase forces $\Theta(m) = m\theta$ and the magnitude forces $R_q(m)R_k(n)$ constant; set both radii to 1 and you have a rotation. A derivation, not a uniqueness theorem — the condition pins down only the product of the radii, and xPos takes that freedom.

A head is 64 or 128 dimensional, so RoPE chops the vector into consecutive pairs and rotates each at its own rate, $\theta_i = \text{base}^{-2i/d}$, with $\text{base} = 10{,}000$ originally. Pair $0$ turns one radian per token; each subsequent pair turns geometrically slower, and the cancellation holds pair by pair.1 RoPE is not one clock but a bank of them.

One practical note, probably a larger part of RoPE's victory than the elegance above: it is $O(d)$ elementwise work leaving nothing inside the attention matrix, so it composes with FlashAttention and a KV cache untouched.2

(a) Position becomes angle. Read two coordinates of a token's vector as a point on a dial. RoPE turns it by an angle proportional to position: one step forward, one turn of $\theta$.
(b) Only the gap survives. Slide the whole sentence one token later and both hands turn together. The shaded gap between them — fixed by how far apart the two tokens are — never changes. The dot product sees only that gap.
Figure 1. Absolute position moves; relative position does not. Watch the wedge in (b), not the arrows — they do the visible work and none of the important work. One band of one head; a real head runs $d/2$ dials at speeds four orders of magnitude apart.

Figure 2. Figure 1 drawn along the sequence rather than on a dial: a token's vector traces a helix as position runs into the page. Slide the query and key together and the wedge never changes; change their separation and it opens.

3. Stretching a Context It Was Never Given

A model trained with a 4,096-token context has seen every angle each band produces inside it and none beyond. The fast bands are unbothered by position 100,000 — they wrap constantly. The slow ones are the problem: never having completed a turn, they are put somewhere genuinely new.

Figure 3. All 64 dials of one head, fastest at the top left. Drag the position and the fast bands blur while the slow ones barely stir — the whole of section 3 in one picture. A dial in rose has never completed a turn.
Figure 4. How many tokens each band needs to return to where it started. The fastest wraps every 6.3 tokens, the slowest needs 54,410, so against a 4,096-token context 18 of 64 never complete a turn. A base of $5\times10^5$ (plum) stretches the slowest to 2.6 million.

Every extension method answers that picture. Position interpolation (Chen et al., 2023) is the blunt one: halve every position index so everything lands back inside the trained range — with a thousand steps of fine-tuning, usually dropped when the method is described. NTK-aware scaling changes the base instead, stretching slow bands more than fast; it appeared in June 2023 as a forum post by a pseudonymous user, bloc97, and was in production before any paper. NTK-by-parts is the three-regime split Figure 4 shades, YaRN adds a temperature on the logits, and LongRoPE searches per-band factors out to 2M tokens. Choosing the base up front differs again: Llama 3 pretrained with 500,000.

All of it asks which clocks to slow down — a coherent question only because RoPE built position out of independent frequencies.

4. A Bound That Falls, and Nothing Underneath It

The paper's second argument got repeated everywhere: setting $\theta_i = \text{base}^{-2i/d}$ gives "a long-term decay property [...], which means the inner-product will decay when the relative position increase."

Figure 5. The paper's evidence. Read the axis label: this is an upper bound on the inner product, not the inner product. It falls from about 20 to 7 across 250 tokens, oscillating the whole way.
Figure 2, Su et al. (2021), recoloured.

A bound that falls obliges nothing underneath to fall. For $q, k$ with independent standard-normal entries the cross terms vanish and the Pythagorean identity leaves

$$ \operatorname{Var}\big(q_m^\top k_n\big) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{d}{2} = d $$

for every relative distance: the typical logit does not decay at all.

Figure 6. Both quantities over the range the paper plots, each divided by its value at distance 10. The bound (lavender) falls to 0.37; the realised logit magnitude for random $q, k$ (blue) sits at 0.98 — flat, as the variance says it must. Decay is not imposed; it is something a model can arrange.

Barbero and colleagues reached the same place in 2024: "A common belief is that RoPE is useful because it helps to decay token dependency as relative distance increases [...] we argue that this is unlikely to be the core reason." Studying Gemma 7B, they found it builds positional patterns from the highest frequencies and leans hardest on the lowest — bands so slow they barely rotate, leaving them free for meaning.

Figure 7. Where the decay comes from. With all 64 bands summed the curve is flat; keep only the fastest and you get a steep, oscillating decay, because fast bands dephase and dephasing makes a sum shrink.

The mechanism is easy to tell backwards. The decay comes from dephasing: at distance 250 the fastest band has turned 250 radians and cancels, while band 63 has turned 0.0 and adds coherently. The fast clocks make the bound fall — so the frequencies Gemma leans on are the ones responsible for whatever decay exists.

None of this is a refutation; the same paper argues for RoPE elsewhere. What survives is narrower: the decay is real as a bound, absent as a typical behaviour, and the frequencies a model leans on are not the ones the original argument predicts. RoPE is a spectrum, and section 3 and this one argue over which parts to use.

5. Chat This Over With Friends

The short version is that RoPE stores position as an angle: rather than adding a position vector to the embedding it rotates the query and the key, chosen so that in the dot product the absolute positions cancel and only the distance survives. The image worth carrying away is that one attention head runs 64 clocks at once at geometrically spaced speeds — the fastest turns fully every 6.3 tokens, the slowest needs 54,410. Against a 4,096-token training window, 18 of those 64 never complete a single turn, which is why a model falls apart past its trained context and why every extension method is a decision about which clocks to slow down.

The claim to be careful with is the one nearly every explanation repeats: that RoPE works because attention decays with distance. The paper does say it, but the decay belongs to an upper bound, and the typical logit is flat — its variance works out to $d$ however far apart the tokens sit. A 2024 study of Gemma argues decay is "unlikely to be the core reason" RoPE helps, finding the model leaning hardest on the slowest bands, which contribute no decay at all. That is one paper on one model family, so the honest version stays narrow: decay is real as a bound, absent as a typical behaviour, and whether a model uses it is the model's own choice. Which is more interesting than the myth it replaces.
