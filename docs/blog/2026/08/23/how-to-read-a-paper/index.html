How to Read a Paper?

Three passes, each one allowed to fail — the method I learned from 李沐, plus a tiered list of where to find papers worth the third pass.

Published

23 August 2026

Read time

14 min read

Tags

Uncategorized

A couple of hundred papers land on arXiv every weekday. So how do you read one?

The best answer I know is not mine. It is 李沐 (Mu Li)'s, and it goes back to a two-page note by S. Keshav from 2007. I met it in a Zhihu write-up, I have used it ever since, and this is my English rendering — all credited at the end.

The idea: read a paper three times, and let yourself stop after any of them.

That last part took me the longest to accept.

One · What a paper is made of

Before the passes, the anatomy. Every paper you open is the same seven parts in the same order, and knowing which part carries what is the whole reason a partial read can be a correct read rather than a lazy one. I ignored this for years, and without it "read less" is only guessing.

Figure 1. The seven parts, and what each one is for. Notice how little of a paper is the method: the claim is made in parts 1 and 3, and paid for in part 6.

Two things I want you to take from that picture:

  • The claim is made three times before you meet any evidence — in the title, in the abstract, and again in the conclusion. Three chances to learn what is being asserted, none of which require you to read the method.
  • The evidence is not in the prose. It is in the figures and tables of the experiments. Most papers' arguments can be checked by looking at four pictures.

So a pass is not "read faster". A pass is read these parts and not those.

Two · The first pass: is this paper for me?

The first pass is a triage, and it should cost you ten to fifteen minutes. Read the title, the abstract, and the conclusion, in that order. Then comes the move most people skip: jump back up into the experiments and look at the key figures and tables. Not read — look.

Figure 2. The first pass, and the move most people miss: after the conclusion you go back up into the experiments and look at the figures. Most papers should leave by the lower exit.

You are answering exactly one question, and it is not is this paper good? It is is this paper for me?

  • The title decides whether it is your area at all. Most rejections happen here and cost four seconds.
  • The abstract gives you the problem, the method in a phrase, and the headline number.
  • The conclusion tells you whether they think they delivered it — and, more usefully, what they admit they did not.
  • The figures are the sanity check. When an abstract promises a large gain and the plot shows two lines you cannot tell apart, you have just learned something the prose was never going to tell you.

Then you either promote the paper to a second pass or you put it down. I put down the large majority, and it took me a long time to stop feeling bad about that. Being able to say "not for me, and here is why" inside fifteen minutes is the skill — not the thing you do instead of it.

Three · The second pass: what does it actually show?

The second pass costs about an hour. Read the whole thing, front to back, at a pace that does not stop.

Skip the proofs. Skip the derivations, skip the hyperparameter appendix. If a formula does not come in about ten seconds, mark it and keep going. It is a pass-three problem, and stopping there is exactly how a second pass turns into a wasted afternoon.

What you do owe your attention to is everything the paper draws.

Figure 3. The second pass has two jobs. On the left, understand the evidence well enough to describe it out loud. On the right, circle the two references that decide what you read next: whoever posed the problem, and whoever owns the method being improved on.

For every figure and every table you should be able to say out loud: what is on the x-axis and in what units, what one point actually represents, which methods are being compared, whether that comparison is fair, and how big the gap really is. If you cannot, you have not read the paper. You have read its adjectives.

The second job of this pass is bibliographic, and it is the one that pays off later. Circle exactly two kinds of reference:

  • the one that posed the problem — where this question came from;
  • the one whose method is being improved on — the paper this one is arguing with.

The rest of the reference list can wait. Those two are the paper's parents, and reading them is usually the cheapest route into the child.

Which gives you the escape hatch, and I wish somebody had told me this at twenty-two. If the second pass leaves you lost — not stuck on one derivation, lost — that is information, not failure. The paper is above your current background, so go and read its parents first. The threshold is lower there, and you come back able to finish.

At the end of a second pass you know what the paper does, roughly how, and roughly how well. For most papers that is where reading them correctly ends, and I think saying so plainly matters: stopping here is not laziness.

Four · The third pass: could I have written it?

The third pass costs hours rather than minutes, so ask once more whether this particular paper has earned it. A paper earns one when you are going to build on it, review it, or argue with it.

The standard now changes completely. In the second pass you needed to know what each section was doing. Here you need to know what each sentence is doing and what each paragraph is for.

And you read it half-closed. As you go, keep trying to rebuild the paper yourself, running a step ahead of the text — guess the next move, then check. Three questions carry that, and the way I remember them is as one question asked into a mirror: everything the author did, reflected back at me.

Figure 4. Three questions, each the author's move reflected back at you: built, tested, further. Ask them in order — the badges light down the mirror in that order, and the glint crossing each card is the reflection doing its work. Answer all three and the paper stops being something you merely consumed.

① Could I have built it? They state a problem and choose a method. Cover the page and ask what you would have reached for. If you would have done the same thing, you have understood the constraint they were under. If you would have done something else, you have just written down a research idea — and you should note why they did not take it, because the answer is usually in the paper.

② Could I have tested it better? They chose those baselines, those datasets, that metric, that ablation. Design the experiment section yourself before you read theirs, then compare. This is where you learn to review, and it is where most of a paper's weaknesses actually live.

③ Could I take it further? Almost every paper ends by admitting what it did not get to. That paragraph is written as an apology. Read it as a to-do list — and one somebody has already told you is worth doing.

Then the honest exam. Shut the paper and rebuild it from memory: the problem, the method, the figure that carries the argument, the number. What you can still draw is what you actually have. What you cannot is where you go back — and now you know exactly which page.

Five · Where to find papers worth a third pass

The method above is a filter, and a filter is only as good as what you point it at. So here is what I actually keep open, ranked — the ordering is by how much reading each one saves me, not by how well known it is. Tier 0 is the firehose; every tier below it is somebody else having already run the first pass on your behalf.

Tier 0 — the raw feed. Ten minutes a day.

  1. Hugging Face Daily Papers — community-voted, and each entry links straight to weights and code. Since Meta sunset Papers with Code on 24 July 2025 this is the de facto replacement; if you keep one tab, keep this one.
  2. arXiv cs.CL and cs.LG new listings — the primary source. Every other item on this page is a filter over these two, so it is worth knowing what the unfiltered version looks like.
  3. alphaXiv — swap arxiv.org for alphaxiv.org in any paper URL and you get a comment layer and a question box beside the actual PDF. Started by Stanford students; raised a $7M seed in November 2025.

Tier 1 — the filter. Someone with taste already did your first pass.

  1. Interconnects — Nathan Lambert, one to three issues a week on post-training, RL and the open-model ecosystem. If you read one newsletter, this is the one whose judgment I trust most on what a result means.
  2. Ahead of AI — Sebastian Raschka. Long, diagrammed walk-throughs of architectures and post-training methods, always citing back to the source so you can go and do your own second pass.
  3. mli/paper-reading (~33k stars) and the 跟李沐学AI channel — line-by-line video readings of the classics, and where the method in this post comes from. Watching somebody do a real third pass on AlexNet or the Transformer once is worth more than any description of one, this post included.
  4. Import AI — Jack Clark, weekly, past 100k subscribers as of January 2026. Strongest on capability trends and policy, which is the context most paper feeds strip out.
  5. AI News — a daily automated sweep of the Discords, X and Reddit. Noisy by design; good for noticing that everyone suddenly started talking about something.

Tier 2 — the tools you open the moment a paper survives pass one.

  1. Semantic Scholar — the citation graph. "Cited by" is how you find out whether a 2024 result actually held up, which no abstract will ever tell you.
  2. Connected Papers — a visual neighbourhood around one paper. Built for precisely the second-pass job of finding who posed the problem.
  3. OpenReview — the reviews and the rebuttals. Reading three rebuttals in your subfield teaches you its standards faster than reading thirty papers does.
  4. Emergent Mind — trending CS papers with generated summaries. Genuinely useful for triage; never let it stand in for a second pass.

Tier 3 — breadth. Skim these; do not subscribe to all of them.

  1. The Batch — weekly, broad, good for the parts of the field you are not in.
  2. Latent Space — the engineering side: what people are shipping with the papers.
  3. 机器之心 and 量子位 — the fastest Chinese-language coverage, and often a day or two ahead on releases from DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi and GLM.
  4. The lab blogs themselves — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Ai2. Lowest volume, highest signal-to-noise, and the place a result usually appears before the paper does.

Now the arithmetic nobody does, which is what actually decides how much of that list you can use.

pass 3 · rebuilt

Steady state, using Keshav's own costs: 10 min a first pass, 1 hour a second, 4 hours a third. The lower bar is where your week's minutes actually go — turn the third-pass share up and watch how fast it eats everything.

At half an hour a day, five days a week — the defaults above — the funnel holds about four papers triaged a week, one second pass, and one paper a month you could rebuild from memory. That number looked far too small to me the first time I worked it out. I have come to think it is roughly right, and that people who appear to read forty papers a week are doing four first passes a day and calling it reading.

Which is fine, as long as you know which pass you are in. That is the part I would keep, if you keep nothing else.

The takeaways
the whole methodRead a paper three times, and let it fail out after any of them. Each pass has its own question, its own budget, and its own exit.
pass one, 15 minutesTitle, abstract, conclusion — then jump back to the figures and tables. One question: is this for me? Most papers should leave here.
pass two, an hourEnd to end, skipping proofs and derivations. Own every axis and every gap, and circle two references: who posed the problem, whose method is being beaten.
when pass two defeats youThat is data, not failure. Go read the paper's parents first — the threshold is lower there — then come back.
pass three, in a mirrorCould I have built it? Could I have tested it better? Could I take it further? Guess the author's next move before reading it.
the only real examShut the paper and rebuild it from memory. What you can still draw is what you have; what you cannot is the page you go back to.
