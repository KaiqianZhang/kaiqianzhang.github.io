A couple of hundred papers land on arXiv every weekday, and I still find
reading even one of them properly hard. The method I lean on is not mine — it
comes from 李沐's 论文精读, and before that a two-page note by S. Keshav, both
credited at the end. The idea: **read a paper three times, and let yourself
stop after any of them.**

## One · What a paper is made of

Before the passes, the anatomy. Every paper you open is the same seven parts
in the same order, and knowing which part carries what is the whole reason a
partial read can be a *correct* read rather than a lazy one. I ignored this
for years, and without it "read less" is only guessing.

Two things I want you to take from that picture:

- **The claim is made three times before you meet any evidence** — in the
  title, in the abstract, and again in the conclusion. Three chances to learn
  what is being asserted, none of which require you to read the method.
- **The evidence is not in the prose.** It is in the figures and tables of the
  experiments. Most papers' arguments can be checked by looking at four
  pictures.

So a pass is not "read faster". A pass is **read these parts and not those**.

## Two · The first pass: is this paper for me?

The **first pass** is a triage, and it should cost you ten to fifteen minutes.
Read the **title**, the **abstract**, and the **conclusion**, in that order.
Then comes the move most people skip: jump *back up* into the experiments and
look at the key figures and tables. Not read — look.

You are answering exactly one question, and it is not *is this paper good?*
It is **is this paper for me?**

- The **title** decides whether it is your area at all. Most rejections happen
  here and cost four seconds.
- The **abstract** gives you the problem, the method in a phrase, and the
  headline number.
- The **conclusion** tells you whether they think they delivered it — and,
  more usefully, what they admit they did not.
- The **figures** are the sanity check. When an abstract promises a large gain
  and the plot shows two lines you cannot tell apart, you have just learned
  something the prose was never going to tell you.

Then you either promote the paper to a second pass or you put it down. I put
down the large majority, and it took me a long time to stop feeling bad about
that. Being able to say *"not for me, and here is why"* inside fifteen minutes
**is** the skill — not the thing you do instead of it.

## Three · The second pass: what does it actually show?

The **second pass** costs about an hour. Read the whole thing, front to back,
at a pace that does not stop.

**Skip the proofs.** Skip the derivations, skip the hyperparameter appendix.
If a formula does not come in about ten seconds, mark it and keep going. It is
a pass-three problem, and stopping there is exactly how a second pass turns
into a wasted afternoon.

What you do owe your attention to is everything the paper *draws*.

For every figure and every table you should be able to say out loud: what is
on the x-axis and in what units, what one point actually represents, which
methods are being compared, whether that comparison is fair, and how big the
gap really is. If you cannot, you have not read the paper. You have read its
adjectives.

The second job of this pass is bibliographic, and it is the one that pays off
later. **Circle exactly two kinds of reference:**

- the one that **posed the problem** — where this question came from;
- the one whose **method is being improved on** — the paper this one is
  arguing with.

The rest of the reference list can wait. Those two are the paper's parents,
and reading them is usually the cheapest route into the child.

Which gives you the escape hatch, and I wish somebody had told me this at
twenty-two. If the second pass leaves you lost — not stuck on one derivation,
*lost* — that is information, not failure. **The paper is above your current
background, so go and read its parents first.** The threshold is lower there,
and you come back able to finish.

At the end of a second pass you know what the paper does, roughly how, and
roughly how well. For most papers that is where reading them correctly ends,
and I think saying so plainly matters: stopping here is not laziness.

## Four · The third pass: could I have written it?

The **third pass** costs hours rather than minutes, so ask once more whether
this particular paper has earned it. A paper earns one when you are going to
build on it, review it, or argue with it.

The standard now changes completely. In the second pass you needed to know
what each *section* was doing. Here you need to know what each *sentence* is
doing and what each paragraph is for.

And you read it half-closed. As you go, keep trying to rebuild the paper
yourself, running a step ahead of the text — guess the next move, then check.
Three questions carry that, and the way I remember them is as one question
asked into a mirror: everything the author did, reflected back at me.

**① Could I have built it?** They state a problem and choose a method. Cover
the page and ask what *you* would have reached for. If you would have done the
same thing, you have understood the constraint they were under. If you would
have done something else, you have just written down a research idea — and
you should note why they did not take it, because the answer is usually in the
paper.

**② Could I have tested it better?** They chose those baselines, those
datasets, that metric, that ablation. Design the experiment section yourself
before you read theirs, then compare. This is where you learn to review, and
it is where most of a paper's weaknesses actually live.

**③ Could I take it further?** Almost every paper ends by admitting what it
did not get to. That paragraph is written as an apology. Read it as a to-do
list — and one somebody has already told you is worth doing.

Then the honest exam. **Shut the paper and rebuild it from memory**: the
problem, the method, the figure that carries the argument, the number. What
you can still draw is what you actually have. What you cannot is where you go
back — and now you know exactly which page.

## Five · Where to find papers worth a third pass

The method above is a filter, and a filter is only as good as what you point
it at. So here is what I actually keep open, ranked — the ordering is by how
much reading each one saves me, not by how well known it is. Tier 0 is the
firehose; every tier below it is somebody else having already run the first
pass on your behalf.

**Tier 0 — the raw feed. Ten minutes a day.**

1. [**Hugging Face Daily Papers**](https://huggingface.co/papers/trending) —
   community-voted, and each entry links straight to weights and code. Since
   Meta sunset Papers with Code on 24 July 2025 this is the de facto
   replacement; if you keep one tab, keep this one.
2. [**arXiv cs.CL**](https://arxiv.org/list/cs.CL/recent) and
   [**cs.LG**](https://arxiv.org/list/cs.LG/recent) new listings — the primary
   source. Every other item on this page is a filter over these two, so it is
   worth knowing what the unfiltered version looks like.
3. [**alphaXiv**](https://www.alphaxiv.org/) — swap `arxiv.org` for
   `alphaxiv.org` in any paper URL and you get a comment layer and a question
   box beside the actual PDF. Started by Stanford students; raised a $7M seed
   in November 2025.

**Tier 1 — the filter. Someone with taste already did your first pass.**

4. [**Interconnects**](https://www.interconnects.ai/) — Nathan Lambert, one to
   three issues a week on post-training, RL and the open-model ecosystem. If
   you read one newsletter, this is the one whose judgment I trust most on
   what a result means.
5. [**Ahead of AI**](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/) — Sebastian
   Raschka. Long, diagrammed walk-throughs of architectures and post-training
   methods, always citing back to the source so you can go and do your own
   second pass.
6. [**mli/paper-reading**](https://github.com/mli/paper-reading) (~33k stars)
   and the [**跟李沐学AI**](https://space.bilibili.com/1567748478) channel —
   line-by-line video readings of the classics, and where the method in this
   post comes from. Watching somebody do a real third pass on AlexNet or the
   Transformer once is worth more than any description of one, this post
   included.
7. [**Import AI**](https://importai.substack.com/) — Jack Clark, weekly, past
   100k subscribers as of January 2026. Strongest on capability trends and
   policy, which is the context most paper feeds strip out.
8. [**AI News**](https://news.smol.ai/) — a daily automated sweep of the
   Discords, X and Reddit. Noisy by design; good for noticing that everyone
   suddenly started talking about something.

**Tier 2 — the tools you open the moment a paper survives pass one.**

9. [**Semantic Scholar**](https://www.semanticscholar.org/) — the citation
   graph. "Cited by" is how you find out whether a 2024 result actually held
   up, which no abstract will ever tell you.
10. [**Connected Papers**](https://www.connectedpapers.com/) — a visual
    neighbourhood around one paper. Built for precisely the second-pass job of
    finding who posed the problem.
11. [**OpenReview**](https://openreview.net/) — the reviews and the rebuttals.
    Reading three rebuttals in your subfield teaches you its standards faster
    than reading thirty papers does.
12. [**Emergent Mind**](https://www.emergentmind.com/) — trending CS papers
    with generated summaries. Genuinely useful for triage; never let it stand
    in for a second pass.

**Tier 3 — breadth. Skim these; do not subscribe to all of them.**

13. [**The Batch**](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/) — weekly, broad,
    good for the parts of the field you are not in.
14. [**Latent Space**](https://www.latent.space/) — the engineering side: what
    people are shipping with the papers.
15. [**机器之心**](https://www.jiqizhixin.com/) and
    [**量子位**](https://www.qbitai.com/) — the fastest Chinese-language
    coverage, and often a day or two ahead on releases from DeepSeek, Qwen,
    Kimi and GLM.
16. **The lab blogs themselves** —
    [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research),
    [OpenAI](https://openai.com/research/),
    [Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/research/) and
    [Ai2](https://allenai.org/blog). Lowest volume, highest signal-to-noise,
    and the place a result usually appears before the paper does.

Now the arithmetic nobody does, which is what actually decides how much of
that list you can use.

At half an hour a day, five days a week — the defaults above — the funnel
holds about **four papers triaged a week, one second pass, and one paper a
month you could rebuild from memory.** That number looked far too small to me
the first time I worked it out. I have come to think it is roughly right, and
that people who appear to read forty papers a week are doing four first passes
a day and calling it reading.

Which is fine, as long as you know which pass you are in. That is the part I
would keep, if you keep nothing else.
