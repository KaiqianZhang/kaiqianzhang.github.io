Here is a fact about language models that I found genuinely surprising the
first time I understood it properly: the mechanism at the heart of a
transformer has no idea what order the words came in.

Shuffle the words of a sentence before feeding them in, and the outputs come
back shuffled the same way, otherwise unchanged. "Dog bites man" and "man
bites dog" are, as far as the machinery is concerned, the same input. If a
model is going to know the difference — and it very much does — somebody has
to arrange that, on purpose, as a separate piece of engineering.

Almost every open model now arranges it the same way, with **rotary position
embedding**, universally called RoPE. What makes it worth a post rather than a
paragraph is that it does not tag a word with where it is. It *rotates* the
word, by an angle proportional to its position, and picks the rotation so that
when two words are compared the two absolute angles cancel and only the
distance between them survives.

I will assume you know nothing about attention and build up to that. By the
end I also want to tell you about the part of the original paper that
everybody repeats and that turns out not to be true.

## A Rotation That Is Thrown Away

In its original formulation RoPE is applied **inside each attention head**, to
queries and keys only, just before the dot product. It never touches the
values or the residual stream: the rotation happens to a copy, is used once,
and is gone — unlike an additive tag, which travels through the whole network.
"Original formulation" does real work there: GPT-J rotates a quarter of each
head's dimensions, DeepSeek's latent attention a dedicated subset, Llama 4
three layers in four. RoPE also has **no learned parameters**, which sounds
like a small virtue and turns out to be the thing section 4 is about.

The 2021 box is where the question gets sharp, and it is worth stating as a
question rather than as an answer, because it is the kind of question that has
the shape of its own solution inside it: **is there a way of writing an
absolute position into a vector such that, when two such vectors are compared,
only the relative position survives?**

That has the ring of something impossible — record where each of two things
is, in a way that reveals only how far apart they are. It is not impossible,
and section 3 is the two lines of algebra that do it.

**Adoption, stated carefully**, because "everything uses RoPE" is the sort of
claim that ages badly. Among dense open-weight decoder-only models released
since 2023 it is close to universal, and it is not universal: BLOOM and MPT
use ALiBi, Jamba uses nothing at all, and Mamba and RWKV are not attention
models, so the question does not arise for them.

## 2. Why Attention Cannot Tell Where Anything Is

I owe you the reason the problem exists, because it is not obvious that it
should.

A word arriving at the model is turned into a **vector**: a list of a few
thousand numbers, learned during training, that stands for that word. The list
depends on the word and on nothing else. Every "bank" in every sentence starts
life as the same list.

What makes a transformer work is **attention**, the operation that lets each
word look at the others and take something from them, so that the "bank" in a
sentence about rivers ends up represented differently from the "bank" in a
sentence about money. The way it decides how much to take is this. Each word
produces a **query**, which you can read as what it is looking for, and a
**key**, which is what it has to offer. To find out how much word $m$ should
take from word $n$, multiply the query of $m$ by the key of $n$ entry by entry
and add the results up — a **dot product**, one number saying how well the two
match. Do that for every pair, turn the numbers into proportions, and blend.

Now look at that procedure and ask where position enters. It does not. The
query depends on the word; the key depends on the word; the dot product
depends on those two and nothing else. Nowhere in the operation is there a
term that knows one word came fourth and the other came ninth. Permute the
input and every pairwise comparison is the same comparison it was, so the
output is simply permuted along with it. Attention treats a sentence as an
unordered collection.

So position has to be injected, and there are exactly two places to put it.
You can put it in the **vectors**, adding something that encodes where the
word sits — the 2017 approach, the top-left box of the roadmap. Or you can put
it in the **comparison**, changing how a query and a key are combined so that
their separation is part of the answer. The first is easy and it leaks: the
position tag rides along with the vector through the entire network,
competing for room with the word's meaning. The second is harder to arrange,
and it is where RoPE lives.

Which brings us back to the question the roadmap ended on, and to what I think
is one of the prettiest answers anywhere in the architecture.

## 3. The Angle That Cancels

Start in two dimensions, where a rotation is something you can picture: take
an arrow, turn it about the origin by some angle, leave its length alone.

With $R(\alpha)$ that rotation and $q = W_q x_m$,
$k = W_k x_n$ the projected query and key, RoPE sets $q_m = R(m\theta)q$ and
$k_n = R(n\theta)k$. Take the dot product, using the two facts that make
rotations special — $R(\alpha)^\top = R(-\alpha)$, and that they compose by
adding angles:

$$
q_m^\top k_n
= q^\top R(-m\theta)R(n\theta)\,k
= q^\top R\big((n-m)\theta\big)\,k .
$$

I would read the middle step slowly, because the whole idea is in it. Undoing
a rotation is rotating backwards, which is why the transpose turns $m\theta$
into $-m\theta$; and rotating by one angle and then another is the same as
rotating once by their sum, which is why the two collapse into a single
rotation by $(n-m)\theta$.

**The absolute positions have cancelled** — exactly, not approximately, not on
average — and they cancelled because of a property that rotations have and
almost nothing else does. Each token was told where it was. Neither can tell
the other, and all that is left in the comparison is how far apart they are.

It is fair to ask where the rotation came from rather than accept being handed
it, and the paper does derive it. Read the pair of numbers as a single complex
number, with a magnitude and a phase. Demand that the inner product depend
only on $n - m$. The phase half of that demand forces the angle to be
proportional to position; the magnitude half forces the product of the two
magnitudes to be constant. Set both to 1 and what remains is a rotation.

That is a derivation and not a uniqueness theorem, and the difference has
consequences. The condition pins down only the *product* of the two
magnitudes, so there is freedom left in how the product is split — and
**xPos** is the method that goes and takes it, letting the magnitudes decay
with distance instead of staying at one.

All of that is in two dimensions, and a real attention head is 64 or 128
dimensional. RoPE handles the gap in the simplest way available: chop the
vector into consecutive pairs, treat each pair as its own two-dimensional
plane, and rotate each at its own rate, $\theta_i = \text{base}^{-2i/d}$, with
$\text{base} = 10{,}000$ in the original. The first pair turns one radian per
token. Each pair after it turns geometrically slower than the last, and
because the cancellation above holds inside each pair separately, it holds for
the sum of all of them.[^sincos]

So **RoPE is not one clock but a bank of them**, all started at the same
moment and running at wildly different speeds. I would fix that image firmly,
because everything remaining in this post is about the bank rather than about
the rotation.

One practical note, which I suspect accounts for more of RoPE's victory than
any of the elegance above. Rotating is $O(d)$ elementwise work, done to the
queries and keys *before* attention runs, and it leaves nothing behind inside
the attention matrix itself. Methods that instead add a learned term to every
entry of that matrix cannot be used with the fused attention kernels
everything now runs on, and sit awkwardly beside a KV cache. RoPE does not
interact with either. It is the rare idea that is both the prettiest available
and the most convenient.[^pairing]

## 4. Stretching a Context It Was Never Given

Here the bank of clocks starts to earn its keep as an image, because it
explains something anybody who uses these models has run into.

A model trained with a 4,096-token context has seen every angle each of its
bands produces inside that window, and no angle beyond it. Ask it about
position 100,000 and the fast bands do not care in the least: they wrap around
constantly, so whatever angle they land on is one they met thousands of times
in training. The slow bands are the problem. Never having completed even one
turn during training, they are being asked for an angle they have genuinely
never seen, and nothing in the model has any reason to know what it means.

Every extension method answers that picture. **Position interpolation** (Chen
et al., 2023) is the blunt one: halve every position index so everything lands
back inside the trained range — with a thousand steps of fine-tuning, usually
dropped when the method is described. **NTK-aware scaling** changes the base
instead, stretching slow bands more than fast; it appeared in June 2023 as a
forum post by a pseudonymous user, bloc97, and was in production before any
paper. **NTK-by-parts** is the three-regime split Figure 4 shades, **YaRN**
adds a temperature on the logits, and **LongRoPE** searches per-band factors
out to 2M tokens. Choosing the base up front differs again: Llama 3 pretrained
with 500,000.

What I would notice is that every one of those methods is asking the same
question: **which clocks should be slowed down, and by how much?** That is a
coherent question to ask only because RoPE built position out of independent
frequencies to begin with. An additive position tag offers nothing to take
hold of. Here the thing you want to stretch arrives pre-separated into parts
that can be stretched by different amounts, which is why a whole research
literature could grow in the gap.

## 5. A Bound That Falls, and Nothing Underneath It

Now the part I most wanted to write, because it is a clean case of a claim
travelling through a field on the strength of being memorable.

The RoPE paper makes a second argument, separate from the cancellation, and
this is the one that got repeated everywhere. Setting
$\theta_i = \text{base}^{-2i/d}$ gives "a long-term decay property [...],
which means the inner-product will decay when the relative position
increase."

            The paper's evidence. Read the axis label: this is an <i>upper bound</i>

        <br>

Read the axis label on that figure before you read anything else about it,
because the quantity plotted is an **upper bound** on the inner product, not
the inner product. A bound that falls obliges nothing underneath it to fall.
The ceiling of a room coming down does not push the furniture through the
floor.

So let us ask what the inner product itself does. For $q$ and $k$ with
independent standard-normal entries — which is what they look like at
initialization — the cross terms vanish, and the Pythagorean identity for sine
and cosine leaves

$$
\operatorname{Var}\big(q_m^\top k_n\big) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{d}{2} = d
$$

for **every** relative distance. The distance does not appear. Two tokens a
thousand apart produce a comparison that is, typically, exactly as large as
one between neighbours. The typical logit does not decay at all — and the
figure below puts the two quantities on the same axes, so you can watch one
fall and the other refuse to.

    <img src='/images/rope-decay-test.png'

    <div class='caption'>
        <span class='caption-label'>Figure 6.</span>
            Both quantities over the range the paper plots, each divided by its

Barbero and colleagues reached the same place in 2024: "A common belief is
that RoPE is useful because it helps to decay token dependency as relative
distance increases [...] we argue that this is unlikely to be the core
reason." Studying Gemma 7B, they found it builds positional patterns from the
**highest** frequencies and leans hardest on the **lowest** — bands so slow
they barely rotate, leaving them free for meaning.

The mechanism is easy to tell backwards, and I told it backwards to myself at
first, so here it is the right way round. The decay in the bound comes from
**dephasing**. At a distance of 250 tokens the fastest band has turned through
250 radians, so its contributions point every which way and cancel one
another; band 63 has turned by essentially nothing, so its contributions all
point the same way and add up. **It is the fast clocks that make the bound
fall, and the slow ones that hold it up.**

Which means the bands Gemma leans on hardest — the slowest ones — are exactly
the bands contributing no decay at all. The model is leaning on the part of
the mechanism that the famous argument is not about.

I want to be careful about how much this overturns, because it is not a
refutation and the same paper argues *for* RoPE elsewhere. What survives is
narrower than "the decay claim is wrong". It is this: the decay is real as a
bound, absent as a typical behaviour, and the frequencies a trained model
leans on are not the ones the original argument would predict. RoPE is a
spectrum, and everything interesting about it — the extension methods of
section 4, the findings here — is a disagreement about which part of that
spectrum does the work.

### Where This Sits Now

If you are heading into research, two things here are worth more than the
mechanism.

The first is the shape of the mistake. A paper proved something true about a
bound, stated it in a sentence that sounded like a statement about behaviour,
and the sentence travelled for three years because it was a satisfying
explanation of why the method worked. It is worth asking, of any claim you
notice yourself repeating, whether you have seen the quantity itself or only
something that sits above it.

The second is that the live work is all in section 4's territory. Context
windows went from 4,096 tokens to hundreds of thousands in about two years,
and almost none of that came from a new position scheme — it came from
retuning this one, band by band, which is why the arguments are about
frequencies rather than about architecture. Meanwhile the fact that a trained
model leans on the lowest frequencies while the design story is about the
highest is exactly the sort of gap between what a mechanism offers and what a
network takes that interpretability work is now built to look for.

## 6. Chat This Over With Friends

The short version is that RoPE stores position as an *angle*: rather than
adding a position vector to the embedding it rotates the query and the key,
chosen so that in the dot product the absolute positions cancel and only the
distance survives. The image worth carrying away is that one attention head
runs 64 clocks at once at geometrically spaced speeds — the fastest turns
fully every 6.3 tokens, the slowest needs 54,410. Against a 4,096-token
training window, 18 of those 64 never complete a single turn, which is why a
model falls apart past its trained context and why every extension method is a
decision about which clocks to slow down.

The claim to be careful with is the one nearly every explanation repeats: that
RoPE works because attention decays with distance. The paper does say it, but
the decay belongs to an *upper bound*, and the typical logit is flat — its
variance works out to $d$ however far apart the tokens sit. A 2024 study of
Gemma argues decay is "unlikely to be the core reason" RoPE helps, finding the
model leaning hardest on the slowest bands, which contribute no decay at all.
That is one paper on one model family, so the honest version stays narrow:
decay is real as a bound, absent as a typical behaviour, and whether a model
uses it is the model's own choice. Which is more interesting than the myth it
replaces.
