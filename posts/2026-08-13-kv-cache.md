I want to explain one of the most consequential ideas in how large language
models are actually run. There is no famous paper behind it. Nobody won a
prize for it, and as far as I can tell nobody ever claimed it as a
contribution. It is in every serious implementation of a language model, it is
the reason a chatbot can answer you at a speed you are willing to sit through,
and it is also the single largest reason that running these models is
expensive and awkward.

It is called the **KV cache**. By the end of this post I would like you to be
able to say exactly what is stored in it, why storing it is allowed at all,
how many bytes it takes, and why the people who run language models for a
living spend so much of their time worrying about it. I am going to assume you
know nothing about how a language model works on the inside, so that is where
we will start.

## The Machine That Starts Over at Every Word

Let me begin with something that surprised me when I first understood it
properly: a language model has no memory.

When you type a question into a chatbot and it writes you an answer, it feels
as though you are talking to something that is following the conversation. I
am afraid you are not, and I still find this a little counterintuitive even
though I know it perfectly well. The thing underneath is a mathematical function in the strict sense.
You hand it a sequence of text, and it hands you back a probability for every
possible word that might come next. That is the whole of what it does. It keeps
no notebook between calls. Everything it appears to remember about your
conversation is there because the whole conversation was handed to it again,
from the beginning, as part of the input.

I want to flag that sentence, because we are going to contradict it later and
I would rather you saw it coming. This post is about a buffer that the model
does keep between calls. The reason that is not a contradiction is that
nothing in the buffer is *new* information: every byte of it could be
recomputed from the text alone. It is a cache, in the ordinary sense of
something kept only to avoid doing work twice, and not a memory.

I should be more careful about the word "word". Language models do not work in
words but in **tokens**, which are chunks of text somewhere between a
character and a word. The word "cache" might be one token; an unusual word
like "Kaiqian" might be broken into three or four. Nothing in this post
depends on the details of how that chunking is done, so if you prefer to
imagine words wherever I write "tokens", you will not go wrong.

So if the model only ever predicts one token, how does it produce a whole
paragraph?

It calls the function again. Let me be concrete about what that means, since
this is the mechanism everything else rests on. The model takes the token it
just produced, glues it onto the end of the text it was given, and feeds the
whole thing back in as if it were a fresh question. Then it does it again. And again. A model writing a
hundred tokens of prose is a function that has been called a hundred times,
each time on a sequence one token longer than the last.

I would like you to sit with that picture for a moment, because the whole of
the rest of this post falls out of it. Every call receives everything the previous call received,
plus one token. By the time the model is writing the thousandth token of an
answer, it is being handed a sequence of a thousand tokens and asked to think
about all of them, when nine hundred and ninety-nine of them are exactly the
same as they were a moment ago.

The obvious question is whether the model really has to think about all of
them again from scratch. The answer is no, and the KV cache is how it avoids
doing so. But to see what can be saved and what cannot, we have to look inside
one of those calls.

The first box is the odd one. There is no citation for it because nobody ever
claimed it as a contribution. Shazeer's 2019 paper prints the algorithm, but
as setup for something else; what it adds is the diagnosis, blaming "the
memory-bandwidth cost of repeatedly loading the large &ldquo;keys&rdquo; and
&ldquo;values&rdquo; tensors".

The third box is the one I like best. It took until 2022 for the thing to
acquire a settled name, in a paper by Pope and colleagues about serving very
large models — and a bottleneck that nobody has a name for is a bottleneck
that is hard to argue about. Within a year of it having one, there was a
serving system organised entirely around managing it.

## 2. Three Vectors, and Which Two of Them Last

To see what is worth keeping, we need to know what the model computes when it
reads a sequence. The mechanism is called **attention**. I have written a
whole post about it — [Attention](/blog/2026/08/13/attention/), which builds it
up from the problem it solves and takes the formula apart term by term — and
if you have never met it, that is the one to read. But I am not going to send
you away to follow this post, so here is what you need, which is less than you
might think.

The problem attention solves is that the meaning of a word depends on the
words around it. In "the cat sat on the mat", the word "sat" means what it
does because of "cat". So each token needs a way to look at the others and
draw in whatever it needs. Attention does this by turning every token into
three lists of numbers rather than one. The first says what that token is
looking for. The second advertises what it has to offer. The third is the
content it hands over if anything wants it. They are called the **query**, the
**key**, and the **value**.

To use them, the current token's query is compared against every key by taking
a **dot product** — multiply the two lists entry by entry and add the results
up — which gives one score per token so far, saying how well each one matches.
Those scores are turned into proportions that add up to one by a function
called the **softmax**, and the proportions are used to blend the value
vectors together. Written down:

$$
\text{Attention}(q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) V .
$$

Two structural facts, because the arithmetic later depends on both. The model
splits the width of its vectors into parallel pieces called **heads**, each
doing all of the above independently with its own learned projections; modern
models use fewer heads for keys and values than for queries, and I will write
$n_{kv}$ for that smaller count. And the whole arrangement repeats at every
**layer**, stacked dozens deep, with Llama 3 70B having 80 of them.

A note on symbols, since I am about to use two that look alike. I write $d_h$
for the width of one head's vectors — how many numbers are in a single key —
and plain $d$ for the full width of the model, all the heads laid end to end.
In Llama 3 8B, $d_h$ is 128 and $d$ is 4,096. Section 5 is
where $d$ turns up.

Now here is the one thing to carry forward, and everything else in this post
follows from it. A token's key and value are computed from that token and the
ones before it, and nothing that arrives later can alter them. Its query is
different in kind: it is the question this token is asking at this moment, it
is used once, and it is never wanted again. Two of the three vectors last, and
one does not.

## 3. The Rows That Never Change Again

Now we can see the waste clearly, and it is embarrassing.

Let me take you back to Figure 1, to the call that produces "mat". That call is handed "the
cat sat on the", and it computes a query, a key and a value for every one of
those five tokens. But the call before it was handed "the cat sat on" and
computed a query, a key and a value for each of those four. The key for "the",
the key for "cat", the key for "sat" — the model computed all of them a moment
ago, threw them away, and has just computed them again. The same numbers.

So keep them. That really is the entire idea, and I remember being faintly
disappointed when I first understood it, because something with such large
consequences ought to be more complicated than this. After computing the key
and value for a token, we put them in a buffer and hold on to them, so that
the next call can use them instead of computing them a second time. That
buffer is the KV cache.

Concretely, for a standard attention layer, the cache is two tensors:

$$
K_{\text{cache}},\; V_{\text{cache}} \in \mathbb{R}^{\,n_{kv} \times t \times d_h},
$$

where $n_{kv}$ is the number of heads that keep keys and values, $t$ is the
number of tokens seen so far, and $d_h$ is the length of each vector. I find
it easiest to read that as a stack of rows: one row per token, for each head,
in each of the two tensors. Every new token adds exactly one row to each. There is one of these
pairs in every layer, and one for every conversation being served.

heads and vectors of length four — absurdly small compared to a real model,
which is the point, because you can see every number. The first two tokens are

      z.push(d / Math.sqrt(DH));
    }

    for (i = 0; i < t; i++) { z[i] = Math.exp(z[i] - m); s += z[i]; }
    return z.map(function (v) { return v / s; });
  }

The counter underneath is the part I would ask you to watch. With a cache,
reaching the fourth token costs four rows of work. Without one it costs nine,
because the model would have redone the whole prefix at every step. Four
against nine is not very impressive on its own, but that gap grows with the
square of the length of the answer, and answers get long.

### The Arithmetic of an Absence

You may have noticed that I said the model computes three vectors per token
but the cache stores two. The queries are thrown away, and this is my
favourite thing in the whole topic, because the reason is so clean.

Think of attention as a question that the present asks of the past. When the
model is working on token $t$, it takes *that* token's query and compares it against
the keys of every token up to and including $t$. It never uses the query
belonging to token 3 again after step 3 has finished. Token 3's key and value,
by contrast, will be consulted at step 4, and step 5, and every step after
that until the conversation ends.

So the asymmetry in the cache is the asymmetry in the question. A token's
question is asked once and is never asked again. A token's answer has to stay
available forever, because everything that comes later is going to ask it
something.

### The Promise That Nothing Will Move

I have been assuming something without saying so, and I want to make it
explicit, because it is the assumption the whole technique stands on. Keeping
old values only works if they are still correct. It would be no use caching
the key for "cat" if appending the word "mat" quietly changed what that key
should have been. So why doesn't it?

In a model of this kind, the key and value for token $i$ are computed from
token $i$ and the tokens before it, and from nothing else. This is a design
choice called **causal masking**: when the model processes a position, it is
forbidden from looking at anything to the right of it. The reason for that
choice has nothing to do with caching — it is there so the model cannot cheat
during training by peeking at the answer — but it has the happy consequence
that appending a token can never reach backwards and change a row that has
already been written.

I want to use this to test a rule you will hear repeated often, which is that
the KV cache only works in decoders. I think that is nearly right and the
reason usually given for it is wrong, which makes it worth taking apart
carefully.

A **bidirectional encoder**, of the kind used in models like BERT, lets every
token look at every other token in both directions. Add a word to the end and
every representation in the sequence changes, because every one of them was
allowed to depend on the whole sequence. There is nothing stable to keep. That
much supports the rule.

But now I want you to consider a translation model, which has an encoder for
the input sentence and a decoder that writes the output. When the decoder attends to the
encoder's output — this is called **cross-attention** — those keys and values
*are* cached, in every real implementation. And cross-attention is
bidirectional. So the rule cannot really be about masking.

What I think the rule is actually about is finality. A row is worth caching
when nothing that arrives later can change it. Causal masking is the usual way to guarantee
that, and it is not the only one: the encoder's output is final too, not
because it is masked, but because the encoder has already finished running.

### Reading Is Not Writing

There is one more distinction I need to give you before we can talk about
cost, and I think it explains most of what is strange about running these
models.

Answering a question happens in two phases that behave nothing alike. The
first is **prefill**: the model reads your entire prompt. It can do this in
one go, because all of the prompt is available at once, and computers are very
good at doing the same operation to a thousand things simultaneously. Prefill
fills the cache and it keeps the hardware busy.

The second is **decode**: the model writes the answer, one token at a time,
because it cannot start the second token until it has seen the first. Each
step does a small amount of arithmetic, and reads the entire cache in order to
attend over it. Prefill is the model reading; decode is the model writing, and
writing is much harder to make fast.

### Counting the Rent

We can now compute the size of the thing. Multiply out the shapes: for $L$
layers, at $p$ bytes for each number,

$$
\text{bytes} \;=\; 2 \, L \, n_{kv} \, d_h \, t \, p .
$$

The 2 is because we keep both a key and a value. Everything else is a count of
how many numbers there are.

Let us put real figures in it. Llama 3 8B, one of the smaller open models, has
$L = 32$ layers, $n_{kv} = 8$ key-value heads, and $d_h = 128$. Models generally store each number in 16 bits rather than
the 32 that used to be standard, because half the precision turns out to be
plenty and it halves the memory. Sixteen bits is two bytes, so $p = 2$. Multiplying it out:
$2 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 131{,}072$ bytes.

That is 128 KiB. I should explain the unit, since it trips people up: memory
is counted in powers of two, so a kibibyte (KiB) is 1,024 bytes rather than
1,000, and 131,072 is exactly $128 \times 1{,}024$. Cache sizes from here on
are in those units. Confusingly, GPUs are *sold* in decimal gigabytes — an
"80 GB" H100 holds 80 billion bytes, which is about 74.5 GiB — so the calculator
just below reports cache sizes in KiB and GiB, while labelling the card it
compares them against in decimal GB, which is how you would find it on a price
list.

I want to let that number sit for a second, because it is *per token*. Every
token in the conversation, whether you typed it or the model wrote it, costs
128 KiB of memory that somebody has to hold onto for as long as the
conversation lasts. Llama 3 70B, with its 80 layers, costs 320 KiB
per token. Models have a limit on how much text they can consider at
once, called the **context window**, and for Llama 3.1 that limit is 128K
tokens — which means 131,072, since these numbers are always powers of two.
Fill it and you are holding exactly 40 GiB of cache. That is for one conversation,
with one user.

## 4. The Bill, Which Is the Other Post

Everything so far has been about what the cache is and why it works. What it
*costs* is a large enough subject that I have given it a post of its
own — [What the KV Cache Costs](/blog/2026/08/13/kv-cache-costs/) — but the
shape of the problem is worth stating here, because it follows directly from
the number we just computed.

The weights of a model are shared. Every conversation on a server runs through
the same ones, so serving many at once spreads their cost across all of them,
which is the entire economics of running these things. The cache is the
opposite: it belongs to one conversation, nobody else can use it, and it grows
for as long as that conversation continues. So the one move that makes
everything else cheaper — serving more people at once — makes this particular
cost worse, because every new person brings another cache to be stored and
read on every step. That tension, and what the field has done about it, is
what the other post is for.

## 5. Fifteen Decimal Places, and a Floor I Did Not Expect

I did not want to take either of the two central claims on trust, so I wrote
the experiment. It decodes 320 tokens through one real attention layer, twice:
once keeping a cache, and once recomputing the whole prefix at every step, the
way a model with no cache would have to. Both paths do genuine arithmetic on
genuine vectors, and I timed each step of both.

The first claim to check is that caching does not change the answer. It should
not — we argued earlier that the rows are final — but arguing and checking are
different activities. Over all 320 steps, the largest disagreement between the
cached output and the recomputed one was $2.2 \times 10^{-15}$, which is the
noise floor of the arithmetic itself. The cache is *algebraically* exact, which
is the sense that matters: it changes no term in the definition of attention.
(Two different implementations on a real GPU, working in 16-bit arithmetic,
will still disagree in the last few bits. That is because floating-point
addition is not associative, so adding the same numbers in a different order
gives a very slightly different answer. It is a property of the arithmetic
rather than of the cache.)

The second claim is about how much work is saved, and here we can be exact,
so let me count it out rather than assert it. I will count multiply-and-add
operations, and I will use $d$ for the full width of the model, not the
per-head $d_h$ from section 2.

A cached step does two things. First it projects the one new token, which
means multiplying its vector of length $d$ by each of four matrices — the
query, key and value projections, and the output projection afterwards. Each
matrix has $d^2$ entries and each entry costs a multiply and an add, so those
four come to $8d^2$. Then it attends over the $t$ rows already sitting in the
cache: one dot product of length $d$ against each of $t$ keys, and then a
weighted sum of $t$ value vectors of length $d$. That is $4td$. So a cached
step costs $8d^2 + 4td$.

A step without a cache does the very same work, but for all $t$ tokens instead
of one. Projecting them all costs $8td^2$, and attending over the full
$t \times t$ grid of scores costs $4t^2d$. Now put the two next to each other. Every term in the second carries exactly
one factor of $t$ that its counterpart in the first does not, so the second is
$t$ times the first — exactly, not approximately. At the 320th token, a model
without a cache is doing three hundred and twenty times the arithmetic to
produce the very same row of numbers. (That is against this particular baseline, which
recomputes the full square of scores; an implementation that exploited the
mask would do about half as much, so call it between $t/2$ and $t$.)

The reason is the subject of the companion post arriving early, on a laptop,
without my arranging it.
A cached step has so little arithmetic to do that the arithmetic is no longer
what is being paid for. What is being paid for is the fixed cost of setting
the work up at all, and one token is nowhere near enough to keep the machine
busy. The cache removes the computation problem so thoroughly that a memory
and overhead problem is all that is left standing. That is the whole of modern
inference engineering in one accidental measurement.

## 6. Chat This Over With Friends

A language model remembers nothing between words. Every token it writes is a
fresh call on the entire conversation so far, and if it really started from
scratch each time, the cost of an answer would grow with the square of its
length. The KV cache is the fix, and it is almost embarrassingly simple: for
each token, the model computes a key and a value once and then keeps them,
because in a causal model nothing that arrives later can change them. The part
I would lead with at a table is what is *not* kept. Each token also produces a
query, and the queries are thrown away — a token's question is asked once and
never again, while its answer has to stay available forever. In a 70B model
each token costs about 320 kilobytes of this, so a single long conversation
quietly accumulates 40 gigabytes of cache, for one person.

The version people repeat is that the KV cache is a decoder-only trick. That
is close, but the reason is not the name of the block: translation models
cache their cross-attention keys quite happily even though cross-attention
looks in both directions, while a bidirectional encoder can cache nothing at
all, because appending a word rewrites every representation it had. What makes
a row keepable is simply that nothing arriving later can change it. The fair
objection to how I have told this is that I have made the saving sound larger
than it is by comparing against a model that recomputes the whole square of
attention scores, when a careful implementation would exploit the mask and do
about half that — the saving is still enormous, but "exactly $t$ times" is the
best case rather than the typical one. What I find genuinely interesting is
that this is one of the few places in the field where the free lunch is real:
the cache changes nothing about what the model says, and every later attempt
to make it cheaper has had to give that up.
