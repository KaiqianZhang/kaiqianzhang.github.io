I want to explain one of the most consequential ideas in how large language
models are actually run. There is no famous paper behind it. Nobody won a
prize for it, and as far as I can tell nobody ever claimed it as a
contribution. It is in every serious implementation of a language model, it is
the reason a chatbot can answer you at a speed you are willing to sit through,
and it is also the single largest reason that running these models is
expensive and awkward.

It is called the **KV cache**. By the end of this post I would like you to be
able to say exactly what is stored in it, why storing it is allowed at all,
how many bytes it takes, and why the people who run language models for a
living spend so much of their time worrying about it. I am going to assume you
know nothing about how a language model works on the inside, so that is where I will start.

## The Machine That Starts Over at Every Word

Let me begin with something that surprised me when I first understood it
properly: a language model has no memory.

When you type a question into a chatbot and it writes you an answer, it feels
as though you are talking to something following the conversation. You are
not. The thing underneath is a mathematical function in the strict sense: you
hand it a sequence of **tokens** — chunks of text between a character and a
word — and it hands back a probability for every token that might come next.

That is the whole of what it does. It keeps no notebook between calls, and
everything it appears to remember is there because the entire conversation was
handed to it again as part of the input.

I want to flag that, because this post is about a buffer the model *does* keep
between calls. It is not a contradiction, and the reason matters: nothing in
that buffer is new information, since every byte of it could be recomputed
from the text alone. It is a cache in the ordinary sense — something kept only
to avoid doing work twice — and not a memory.

So how does a model that predicts one token produce a paragraph? It calls the
function again, having glued its own output onto the end of the input. Then
again, and again. A model writing a hundred tokens is a function called a
hundred times, each time on a sequence one token longer than the last.

The obvious question is whether the model really has to think about all of
them again from scratch. The answer is no, and the KV cache is how it avoids
doing so. But to see what can be saved and what cannot, you have to look inside one of
those calls.

## 2. Three Vectors, and Which Two of Them Last

To see what is worth keeping, we need to know what the model computes when it
reads a sequence. The mechanism is called **attention**.

I have written a whole post about it — [Attention](/blog/2026/06/24/attention/),
which builds it up from the problem it solves and takes the formula apart term
by term — and if you have never met it, that is the one to read. But I am not
going to send you away to follow this post, so here is what you need, which is
less than you might think.

The problem attention solves is that the meaning of a word depends on the
words around it. In "the cat sat on the mat", the word "sat" means what it
does because of "cat". So each token needs some way to look at the others and
draw in whatever it needs from them, and that is all I am going to claim for
it here.

Attention does this by turning every token into three lists of numbers rather
than one. The first says what that token is looking for. The second advertises
what it has to offer. The third is the content it hands over if anything wants
it. They are called the **query**, the **key**, and the **value**.

To use them,
compare the current token's query against every key by taking a **dot
product** — multiply the two lists entry by entry and add the results
up — which gives one score per token so far, saying how well each one
matches. We then turn those scores into proportions that add up to one, using
a function called the **softmax**, and use the proportions to blend the value
vectors together. Written down:

$$
\text{Attention}(q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) V .
$$

Two structural facts, because the arithmetic later depends on both. The model
splits the width of its vectors into parallel pieces called **heads**, each
doing all of the above independently with its own learned projections; modern
models use fewer heads for keys and values than for queries, and I will write
$n_{kv}$ for that smaller count. And the whole arrangement repeats at every
**layer**, stacked dozens deep, with Llama 3 70B having 80 of them.

A note on symbols, since I am about to use two that look alike. I write $d_h$
for the width of one head's vectors — how many numbers are in a single key —
and plain $d$ for the full width of the model, all the heads laid end to end.
In Llama 3 8B, $d_h$ is 128 and $d$ is 4,096. Section 5 is
where $d$ turns up.

Now here is the one thing I would ask you to carry forward, because everything
else in this post follows from it. A token's key and value are computed from
that token and the ones before it, and nothing arriving later can alter them.
Its query is different in kind: it is the question this token is asking at
this moment, it is used once, and it is never wanted again. Two of the three
vectors last, and one does not.

## 3. The Rows That Never Change Again

Now you can see the waste clearly, and it is embarrassing.

Let me take you back to Figure 1, to the call that produces "mat". That call
is handed "the cat sat on the", and it computes a query, a key and a value for
every one of those five tokens.

But the call before it was handed "the cat sat on" and computed a query, a key
and a value for each of those four. The key for "the", the key for "cat", the
key for "sat" — the model computed all of them a moment ago, threw them away,
and has just computed them again. The same numbers.

So keep them. That really is the entire idea, and I remember being faintly
disappointed when I first understood it, because something with such large
consequences ought to be more complicated than this. After computing the key and value for a token, the
model puts them in a buffer and holds on to them, so that
the next call can use them instead of computing them a second time. That
buffer is the KV cache.

Concretely, for a standard attention layer, the cache is two tensors:

$$
K_{\text{cache}},\; V_{\text{cache}} \in \mathbb{R}^{\,n_{kv} \times t \times d_h},
$$

where $n_{kv}$ is the number of heads that keep keys and values, $t$ is the
number of tokens seen so far, and $d_h$ is the length of each vector. I find
it easiest to read that as a stack of rows: one row per token, for each head,
in each of the two tensors. Every new token adds exactly one row to each. There is one of these
pairs in every layer, and one for every conversation being served.

Let me show you it filling up. Below is a walkthrough of four tokens, with two
heads and vectors of length four — absurdly small compared to a real model,
which is the point, because you can see every number. The first two tokens are
the prompt and the last two are generated. Step through it and watch the rows
accumulate.

### The Arithmetic of an Absence

You may have noticed that I said the model computes three vectors per token
but the cache stores two. The queries are thrown away, and this is my
favourite thing in the whole topic, because the reason is so clean.

I think of attention as a question that the present asks of the past. When the
model is working on token $t$, it takes *that* token's query and compares it against
the keys of every token up to and including $t$. It never uses the query
belonging to token 3 again after step 3 has finished. Token 3's key and value,
by contrast, will be consulted at step 4, and step 5, and every step after
that until the conversation ends.

So the asymmetry in the cache is really the asymmetry in the question, which
is why I find it such a satisfying piece of design. A token's question is
asked once and never again. Its answer has to stay available forever, because
everything that comes later is going to ask it something.

### The Promise That Nothing Will Move

I have been assuming something without saying so, and I want to make it
explicit, because it is the assumption the whole technique stands on. Keeping
old values only works if they are still correct. It would be no use caching
the key for "cat" if appending the word "mat" quietly changed what that key
should have been. So why doesn't it?

The answer is a design choice called **causal masking**. In a model of this
kind, the key and value for token $i$ are computed from token $i$ and the
tokens before it and from nothing else, because when the model processes a
position it is forbidden from looking at anything to the right of it.

The reason for that choice has nothing to do with caching — it is there so the
model cannot cheat during training by peeking at the answer — but it has the
happy consequence that appending a token can never reach backwards and change
a row that has already been written.

I want to use this to test a rule you will hear repeated often, which is that
the KV cache only works in decoders. I think that is nearly right and the
reason usually given for it is wrong, which makes it worth taking apart
carefully.

Take a **bidirectional encoder**, of the kind used in models like BERT, which
lets every token look at every other token in both directions. Add a word to the end and
every representation in the sequence changes, because every one of them was
allowed to depend on the whole sequence. There is nothing stable to keep. That
much supports the rule.

But now I want you to consider a translation model, which has an encoder for
the input sentence and a decoder that writes the output. When the decoder attends to the
encoder's output — this is called **cross-attention** — those keys and values
*are* cached, in every real implementation. And cross-attention is
bidirectional. So the rule cannot really be about masking.

What I think the rule is actually about is finality. A row is worth caching
when nothing that arrives later can change it. Causal masking is the usual way to guarantee
that, and it is not the only one: the encoder's output is final too, not
because it is masked, but because the encoder has already finished running.

### Reading Is Not Writing

There is one more distinction I need to give you before I can talk about cost, and I think it explains most of what is strange about running these
models.

Answering a question happens in two phases that behave nothing alike, and I
want to name them both because the difference between them is what makes
serving these models strange. The first is **prefill**: the model reads your
entire prompt. It can do this in one go, because all of the prompt is
available at once and computers are very good at doing the same operation to a
thousand things simultaneously. Prefill fills the cache, and it keeps the
hardware busy.

The second is **decode**: the model writes the answer one token at a time,
because it cannot begin the second token until it has seen the first. Each
step does a very small amount of arithmetic and yet still has to read the
entire cache in order to attend over it.

I would hold those two facts side by side for a moment, because the imbalance
between them is what the companion post is about. Prefill is the model reading
and decode is the model writing, and writing is far harder to make fast.

### Counting the Rent

Now we can compute the size of the thing. Multiply out the shapes: for $L$
layers, at $p$ bytes for each number,

$$
\text{bytes} \;=\; 2 \, L \, n_{kv} \, d_h \, t \, p .
$$

The 2 is because we keep both a key and a value. Everything else is a count of
how many numbers there are.

Let me put real figures in it. Llama 3 8B, one of the smaller open models, has
$L = 32$ layers, $n_{kv} = 8$ key-value heads, and $d_h = 128$. Models generally store each number in 16 bits rather than
the 32 that used to be standard, because half the precision turns out to be
plenty and it halves the memory. Sixteen bits is two bytes, so $p = 2$. Multiplying it out:
$2 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 131{,}072$ bytes.

That is 128 KiB. I should explain the unit, since it trips people up: memory
is counted in powers of two, so a kibibyte (KiB) is 1,024 bytes rather than
1,000, and 131,072 is exactly $128 \times 1{,}024$. Cache sizes from here on
are in those units.

Confusingly, GPUs are *sold* in decimal gigabytes — an "80 GB" H100 holds 80
billion bytes, which is about 74.5 GiB — so the calculator just below reports
cache sizes in KiB and GiB, while labelling the card it compares them against
in decimal GB, which is how you would find it on a price list.

I want to let that number sit for a second, because it is *per token*. Every
token in the conversation, whether you typed it or the model wrote it, costs
128 KiB of memory that somebody has to hold onto for as long as the
conversation lasts. Llama 3 70B, with its 80 layers, costs 320 KiB per token.

Models have a limit on how much text they can consider at once, called the
**context window**, and for Llama 3.1 that limit is 128K tokens — which means
131,072, since these numbers are always powers of two. Fill it and you are
holding exactly 40 GiB of cache. That is for one conversation, with one user.

That number is why anybody worries about this at all.

## 4. The Bill, Which Is the Other Post

Everything so far has been about what the cache is and why it works. What it
*costs* is a large enough subject that I have given it a post of its
own — [What the KV Cache Costs](/blog/2026/08/12/kv-cache-costs/) — but the
shape of the problem is worth stating here, because it follows directly from the number just computed.

Here is the shape of it. The weights of a model are shared: every conversation
on a server runs through the same ones, so serving many at once spreads their
cost across all of them, which is the entire economics of running these
things. The cache is the opposite: it belongs to one conversation, nobody else
can use it, and it grows for as long as that conversation continues.

So the one move that makes everything else cheaper — serving more people at
once — makes this particular cost worse, because every new person brings
another cache to be stored and read on every step. That tension, and what the
field has done about it, is what the other post is for.

## 5. Fifteen Decimal Places, and a Floor I Did Not Expect

I did not want to take either of the two central claims on trust, so I wrote
the experiment. It decodes 320 tokens through one real attention layer, twice:
once keeping a cache, and once recomputing the whole prefix at every step, the
way a model with no cache would have to. Both paths do genuine arithmetic on
genuine vectors, and I timed each step of both.

The first claim to check is that caching does not change the answer. It should not — I argued earlier that the rows are final — but arguing and checking are
different activities. Over all 320 steps, the largest disagreement between the
cached output and the recomputed one was $2.2 \times 10^{-15}$, which is the
noise floor of the arithmetic itself.

The cache is *algebraically* exact, which is the sense that matters: it
changes no term in the definition of attention. (Two different implementations
on a real GPU, working in 16-bit arithmetic, will still disagree in the last
few bits. That is because floating-point addition is not associative, so
adding the same numbers in a different order gives a very slightly different
answer. It is a property of the arithmetic rather than of the cache.)

The second claim is about how much work is saved. Counting multiply-and-adds,
a cached step costs $8d^2 + 4td$ and an uncached one costs $8td^2 + 4t^2d$,
writing $d$ for the full width of the model. Every term in the second carries
exactly one factor of $t$ that its counterpart in the first does not, so the
second is $t$ times the first — exactly, not approximately.

At the 320th token, a model without a cache does three hundred and twenty
times the arithmetic to produce the very same row of numbers. (That is against
this particular baseline, which recomputes the full square of scores; an
implementation exploiting the mask would do about half as much, so call it
between $t/2$ and $t$.)

Panel (a) is the one I did not expect, and it turned out to be the most
instructive thing in the experiment. The blue points, the ones without a
cache, land neatly on the curve their arithmetic predicts. The lavender
points, with the cache, refuse to. They sit flat, and far above their own
prediction — roughly ten times higher than the FLOP count says they should be.

The reason is the subject of the companion post arriving early, on a laptop,
without my arranging it. A cached step has so little arithmetic to do that the
arithmetic is no longer what is being paid for.

What is being paid for is the fixed cost of setting the work up at all, and
one token is nowhere near enough to keep the machine busy. The cache removes
the computation problem so thoroughly that a memory and overhead problem is
all that is left standing. That is the whole of modern inference engineering
in one accidental measurement.

### Where This Sits Now

If you are heading into research, the thing to carry from this is that the
cache turned a compute problem into a memory problem, and the field is still
living with that trade.

Everything current follows from it: the prefill-and-decode split has become an
argument for running those two phases on *different machines*, since one is
compute-bound and the other is not; architectures are being redesigned around
what they have to store rather than what they have to compute; and the long-
context work that everyone wants is gated less by whether a model can attend
over a million tokens than by whether anybody can afford to keep the rows.

## 6. Chat This Over With Friends

A language model remembers nothing between words. Every token it writes is a
fresh call on the entire conversation so far, and if it really started from
scratch each time, the cost of an answer would grow with the square of its
length. The KV cache is the fix, and it is almost embarrassingly simple: for
each token, the model computes a key and a value once and then keeps them,
because in a causal model nothing that arrives later can change them. The part
I would lead with at a table is what is *not* kept. Each token also produces a
query, and the queries are thrown away — a token's question is asked once and
never again, while its answer has to stay available forever. In a 70B model
each token costs about 320 kilobytes of this, so a single long conversation
quietly accumulates 40 gigabytes of cache, for one person.

The version people repeat is that the KV cache is a decoder-only trick. That
is close, but the reason is not the name of the block: translation models
cache their cross-attention keys happily even though cross-attention
looks in both directions, while a bidirectional encoder can cache nothing at
all, because appending a word rewrites every representation it had. What makes
a row keepable is simply that nothing arriving later can change it. The fair
objection to how I have told this is that I have made the saving sound larger
than it is by comparing against a model that recomputes the whole square of
attention scores, when a careful implementation would exploit the mask and do
about half that — the saving is still enormous, but "exactly $t$ times" is the
best case rather than the typical one. What I find genuinely interesting is
that this is one of the few places in the field where the free lunch is real:
the cache changes nothing about what the model says, and every later attempt
to make it cheaper has had to give that up.
